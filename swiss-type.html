Swiss-type turning: why the guide bushing decides what you can hold

Notes from the turning floor of CHX Precision Metal, Shenzhen — 70 Tsugami sliding-headstock lathes running Ø1–20 mm parts.

Short answer: on a Swiss-type lathe the bar is pushed through a carbide guide bushing that sits millimetres from the cutting edge, so the workpiece is supported where it is being cut. That single detail is why a Ø3 mm pin, 60 mm long, comes off a Swiss machine straight while the same part on a fixed-head lathe walks away from the tool. It is also why the process has a hard ceiling around Ø20–32 mm on most machines and cannot be pushed much beyond a 4:1 or 5:1 length-to-diameter ratio in one pass without support.

What actually differs from a fixed-head lathe

On a conventional lathe the bar is held in a chuck at one end and the tool works on the other. The further the tool gets from the chuck, the more the part deflects — cutting force pushes it away, the tool stops removing the intended depth of cut, and the diameter comes out tapered. Machinists compensate by taking lighter passes, using a tailstock, or accepting a slower cycle.

On a Swiss-type lathe the Z axis moves the bar, not the tool. The bar slides through a guide bushing and the tools work right at the bushing face. Deflection is limited to the few millimetres of material between the bushing and the cut, which is small enough that the part does not move measurably. The part is also turned in one continuous pass along its length, so a 60 mm pin is machined as a 60 mm pin rather than as a sequence of overhung cuts.

Two consequences follow, and both matter to a buyer:

  • Long, thin parts hold size and straightness that a fixed-head lathe cannot match — this is the whole reason the process exists.
  • Because the bar is guided, the maximum diameter is limited by the bushing and the machine's bar feeder, not by a chuck. A 20 mm machine cannot take a 25 mm bar, full stop.

When Swiss-type is the right choice

Three signals point to Swiss:

  1. The diameter is under 20 mm and the part is longer than it is wide. A Ø4 mm shaft at 40 mm long is a Swiss part. The same shaft at 8 mm long is a bar-fed lathe part, and the choice becomes about volume rather than geometry.
  2. The part has features on both ends. A sub-spindle picks up the part and works the back side without a second operation, which removes one handling step and one opportunity for a scratch or a burr.
  3. The annual volume is in the thousands, not the dozens. Swiss machines change over quickly within a family of similar parts, but programming and setup still have to be absorbed. At 50 pieces a year, the economics rarely work in the part's favour.

The reverse also holds. Large flanges, housings, anything over roughly 32 mm in diameter, and short stubby parts with a low length-to-diameter ratio are not Swiss work — they belong on a lathe with a chuck or on a machining centre.

The tolerance question, answered plainly

A Swiss lathe does not automatically hold a tighter tolerance than any other machine. What it does is remove deflection as a source of error, which makes a tight tolerance repeatable across a production run. A shop quoting ±0.005 mm on a Ø6 mm pin is making a different claim than a shop quoting ±0.005 mm on a Ø6 mm pin 80 mm long: the first is routine, the second depends on support, tool wear and how the part is measured.

So the useful question is not "what tolerance can you hold" but "on this dimension, on this part, with this setup — what do you expect the spread to be, and how will you measure it". A shop that answers in numbers has programmed the part. A shop that answers with a brochure number has not.

What to put on the drawing

  • Bar diameter, not just finished diameter. A Ø12 mm part can be cut from Ø12 mm bar or from Ø14 mm bar with more stock. The two are different prices and different cycle times.
  • Tolerance per feature. A blanket ±0.005 mm across every dimension on a part that needs it on two features doubles the price for no benefit. Mark the critical ones and let the rest follow ISO 2768-m.
  • Which end is the datum. On a sub-spindle part, the datum for the second operation has to be defined or the two ends will not agree.
  • Burr and edge condition. "Deburr all edges" is not a specification. Say whether a 0.05 mm radius is acceptable, or whether the edge has to be sharp and clean for a press fit.
  • Material condition. 303 and 316L machine differently; 316L work-hardens and rewards a shop that keeps the tool engaged. Say which grade and which condition.

How the volume economics work

Swiss turning wins on cost per piece at volume because several tools cut at once and the part is finished in one handling. On a 20,000-piece order of a Ø5 mm connector body, the difference against a turret lathe running the same part can exceed 30 percent, driven by cycle time rather than material.

At low volume the picture flips. Setup, programming and first-article inspection are fixed costs, and the cam layout or programme has to be proven before the first good part. That is why a 1-piece order is possible but priced accordingly, and why the piece price stops falling at some quantity — the point where setup has been fully amortised. That quantity is the number worth asking for.
